The pattern hiding in the numbers

def make_forecast_ready(path, freq="MS", lower_q=0.02, upper_q=0.98):
    data = pd.read_csv(path, parse_dates=["DATE"]).set_index("DATE")
    full_calendar = pd.date_range(data.index.min(), data.index.max(), freq=freq)

    series = data["Sales"].interpolate(method="linear")

    return series.asfreq(freq).rename("Sales")

2 · Smoothing

Moving averages with rolling()

Instead of looking at one noisy month, average the last few months. A window of fixed size 𝑚 slides one step at a time, which is why it is called a moving average. Think of a cricket batter's "last 10 innings average": it reacts to a change in form but does not jump after one big score or one duck. In pandas the tool is rolling() followed by an operation such as mean().

ŷt = (1/𝑚) · Σj=1..𝑚 yt−j

Which points does the window use for the value at 𝑡?

Moving-average explorer

Visualizer · rolling window lab
Simulated accessories series from the class (Section 6). Try window 1, 3, 12 and 24, and switch the window type.

ma3 = sales.rolling(3).mean()
sales.rolling(12).mean()                    # trailing
sales.rolling(12, center=True).mean()       # centered

The window of 3 follows the series closely and still shows the yearly zig-zag, while the window of 12 is almost a smooth upward line. Two costs come with a bigger window: more empty values at the start (the window size minus one for a trailing window) and more lag, meaning the average turns later than the data. A window of 1 gives back the original series exactly.

Why does a window of 12 make sense for monthly data?
One window of 12 covers every calendar month exactly once, so the yearly pattern cancels out and mostly the slow movement remains.

Weighted moving average (WMA)

A simple moving average treats last month and three months ago alike. A weighted moving average gives recent months more weight, and divides by the sum of weights so the result stays on the same scale.

ŷt = Σ aj yt−j / Σ aj,   a1 > a2 > … > am
weights = np.array([1, 2, 3])        # oldest ... newest inside each window of 3
wma3 = sales.rolling(3).apply(lambda x: np.dot(x, weights) / weights.sum(), raw=True)

The newest month gets three times the weight of the oldest, divided by 6. On the MobiPlus series the WMA stays closer to the actual values than the SMA, and in the last rows, where sales rise, it reacts faster. (The explorer above uses weights 1, 2, …, 𝑚.) Choosing the weights by hand is arbitrary, and nothing stops us from letting a computer choose them: that is the seed of auto-regression, explored in later classes.

Centered moving average (CMA)

A centered window takes points on both sides of the current one. In pandas it is one argument: center=True.

ŷt = 1/(2𝑚+1) · Σj=t−m..t+m yj

The centered line sits on the middle of the series, while the trailing one runs late by about half a window. A window of 12 is even, so there is no single middle month, and pandas leaves 6 empty values at the start and 5 at the end (try it in the explorer).

ImportantA centered average needs future values, so it can be used to smooth and to explain history but never to forecast. It is a preprocessing and analysis tool.

Link back to Class 1: interpolation is a centered average

Hide one month and fill it two ways: with s.interpolate() and with s.rolling(3, center=True, min_periods=2).mean(). Both fills are the average of the two neighbours, so linear interpolation over a single gap is a centered moving average whose window is one point on each side.

Practice · Which window size?

The window size is a hyperparameter: too small keeps the noise, too large erases real changes. 3 · Trend

The slow movement

The trend is the slow, long-term movement of a series: upward, downward, or changing direction over time. It does not repeat the way a season does. A song may trend for a few weeks and then disappear. A trend line is a smooth function that traces this movement; the word "line" is misleading, because it can curve. A series has one trend: if several movements exist they combine into a single resulting curve.

Way 1: a long rolling average

A long centered window removes noise and seasonality, and what is left is the trend. For monthly data the window should be at least one full year.

Way 2: fit a regression on time

Time is the only input, so with 𝑡 = 0, 1, 2, … the straight-line trend is ŷt = w·t + c. A curved trend uses a polynomial of degree 2. numpy.polyfit fits any degree.

t = np.arange(len(sales))
lin  = np.polyval(np.polyfit(t, sales.values, 1), t)
quad = np.polyval(np.polyfit(t, sales.values, 2), t)
Rolling averageFollows the curve of the data but loses values at both ends. Says "here is what happened".
Regression on timeGives a formula that extends to every month and can be read directly. Says "here is a rule", which is why it can later be extended into the future.

On the MobiPlus series the two ways tell almost the same story: sales grow by about 39 phones per month, and the quadratic fit stays within about 11 phones of the straight line, so a straight line is a good description of this series.

The one constraint: a trend cannot be periodic

If the estimated "trend" still goes up and down every year, it is not a pure trend: seasonality has leaked into it.

Visualizer · trend window lab
Centered rolling averages of the simulated accessories series, 2014–2018. Hover to compare.
Average month-to-month swing of each trend estimate (whole series)

A smaller swing means a smoother estimate. A window shorter than the cycle keeps repeating the yearly wave, so it is not a valid trend. The rule: if the trend is periodic, move the seasonality out of it first, which the next sections show.

4 · Seasonality

Patterns with a fixed period

Seasonality is a pattern that repeats at a fixed and known period. It can be seen as a simple repeating wave, and the repeatability is exactly what makes forecasting possible. You recognise it by regularly spaced peaks and troughs with a consistent direction and roughly the same size every cycle, relative to the trend.

TypeExample for MobiPlusPeriod
HourlyStore visits peak in the evening24 hours
WeeklyHigher footfall on weekends7 days
YearlyFestive season in October to December12 months
Weather-linkedSweaters in winter, sunglasses in summer12 months

How to compute it

  1. Estimate the trend and subtract it from the series.
  2. Group what is left by position in the cycle (all Januaries, all Februaries and so on) and average each group.
  3. Shift the averages so they add up to zero.
Visualizer · seasonality step by step
Illustrated on the simulated accessories series, with a 2×12 centered average as the trend.

detrended = sales - trend12
seasonal_by_month = detrended.groupby(detrended.index.month).mean()
seasonal_by_month = seasonal_by_month - seasonal_by_month.mean()

On the MobiPlus series the bars say that in an average year January and February are the weakest months, about 1,000 and 1,700 phones below the trend, and November and December lift sales by about 950 and 1,500 phones. This is the repeating pattern from Class 1, now measured in phones.

If next year's trend is 14,000 phones a month, what would you expect December to be?
About 14,000 plus the December effect. Seasonality is added on top of the trend.

More than one seasonality

Real series often carry two cycles at once. Here is a simulated daily footfall series for a MobiPlus store, with a weekly cycle (busy weekends) and a yearly cycle (festive months).

The zoom shows a weekly ripple with Saturday as the peak; the full view shows a slow yearly wave. One series, two periods: 7 and about 365. A good analysis names both, and they are decomposed one after the other.

How do I know the period?
Domain knowledge first: days of the week, months of the year, hours of the day. When unsure, plot the series, zoom in, and look at how far apart the repeating peaks are.
5 · Decomposition

Additive decomposition and the residual term

A series is made of simple components that can be estimated one by one:

y(t) = b(t) + s(t) + e(t)   trend + seasonality + residual

Here 𝑏(𝑡) is the trend, 𝑠(𝑡) is the seasonality and 𝑒(𝑡) is the error or residual: whatever the first two cannot explain. This is the additive model, and the residual is not estimated, it is computed:

e(t) = y(t) − b(t) − s(t)

Think of a cup of tea: the water is the trend, the sugar is the seasonality, and the leftover bits that settle at the bottom are the residual.

For an even period such as 12 there is no single middle month, so the trend uses a centered window of 13 points with half weight on both ends, often written 2×12 MA.

Visualizer · why 2×12
Weights given to each month around the centre point. The plain 12-point window cannot be centered on a single month; 2×12 puts half weight on both ends.
def centered_ma(s, period):
    if period % 2 == 1:
        return s.rolling(period, center=True).mean()
    w = np.r_[0.5, np.ones(period - 1), 0.5] / period          # 2 x period moving average
    return s.rolling(period + 1, center=True).apply(lambda x: np.dot(x, w), raw=True)

def decompose(s, period, model="additive"):
    trend = centered_ma(s, period)
    pos = np.arange(len(s)) % period                           # position inside the cycle
    if model == "additive":
        raw = (s - trend).groupby(pos).mean();  raw = raw - raw.mean()
        seasonal = pd.Series(raw.loc[pos].values, index=s.index)
        resid = s - trend - seasonal
    else:
        raw = (s / trend).groupby(pos).mean();  raw = raw / raw.mean()
        seasonal = pd.Series(raw.loc[pos].values, index=s.index)
        resid = s / (trend * seasonal)
    return pd.DataFrame({"observed": s, "trend": trend, "seasonal": seasonal, "resid": resid})

Decomposing gives four panels: the observed series, a smooth rising trend, a seasonal pattern that repeats exactly every year, and a residual hovering around zero. Adding the first three components back reproduces the observed series up to the residual, by construction. On MobiPlus the residual mean is about 0.6 (essentially zero next to sales of about 10,000) and its spread about 430 phones, a small share of the level. A lopsided histogram would hint that a constant term is missing; a narrow bell shape is what we hope for.

When the residual still shows a pattern

Sometimes the residual keeps a trend or seasonality. This happens when the series has more than one seasonality or when the trend was estimated wrongly. Test it on the daily footfall series by decomposing only the weekly cycle.

Visualizer · nested decomposition
30-day average of the residual of daily footfall, same scale for both lines.
Spread (standard deviation) of the footfall series

After the weekly step a slow yearly wave is still visible: pattern is left. The fix is to treat the residual as a new series and decompose it again. The spread falls from about 26.7 to 3.7 to 2.6, below the noise of about 4 that was added: with only three years of data each calendar day occurs about twice, so the yearly averages absorb part of the noise, one reason long cycles need many years of history.

yA(t) = bA(t) + sA(t) + bB(t) + sB(t) + eB(t)  series B is the residual of series A

The process continues until the residual has no pattern.

6 · Decomposition

Multiplicative decomposition and the log link

In the MobiPlus series the seasonal swing stays about the same size every year. In many businesses the swing grows with the level: when sales are twice as large, the December jump is twice as large. A simulated MobiPlus accessories line shows this. Adding a fixed seasonal pattern cannot represent it: the same +300 would be big early and negligible later. The idea is to multiply:

y(t) = b(t) × s(t) × e(t),   e(t) = y(t) / (b(t)·s(t))

Now 𝑠(𝑡) is a factor around 1: a December factor of 1.3 means "30% above the trend", whatever the level is.

Visualizer · decomposition explorer
Simulated accessories sales, 2010–2018

add = decompose(accessories, 12, "additive")
mul = decompose(accessories, 12, "multiplicative")

The yearly peaks get taller as the series grows. With the additive model the residual spread grows by about 40% from the early to the late years (110 to 157 units), which means the swing left behind grows with the level. With the multiplicative model the residual, expressed as a ratio, stays at about 2% of the level in both periods: the sign of a model that fits.

The log link

Taking logarithms turns a product into a sum:

log y(t) = log b(t) + log s(t) + log e(t)

So a multiplicative model is an additive model on the log of the series. Decomposing np.log(accessories) additively and converting back with exp gives nearly the same twelve seasonal factors:

Visualizer · two routes, same factors

The log link is not just a formula: it lets every additive tool be reused on a multiplicative series, then converted back with exp. In practice, if the swing grows with the level, either multiply or log-transform first.

How do I choose between additive and multiplicative?
Plot the series. If the seasonal swing stays about the same height, use additive. If it grows or shrinks with the level, use multiplicative. If the series contains zeros or negative numbers, multiplication and logs are a problem, so stay additive.
7 · In practice

statsmodels seasonal_decompose and decomposition from scratch

We wrote the decomposition by hand to understand it. In practice one line does the same job:

result = sm.tsa.seasonal_decompose(sales, model="additive", period=12)
result.plot()

The four panels are the observed series, the trend (empty at both ends because statsmodels also uses a centered average), the seasonality and the residual (result.resid). Our function and statsmodels agree to many decimal places for trend, seasonality and residual, so there is no magic inside the library: it is the 2×12 centered average, the monthly averages and a subtraction.

A common stumbleUsing plain rolling(12, center=True) for the trend and wondering why the numbers differ slightly from statsmodels. The plain 12-point window cannot be centered on a single month, so it lands half a month off (see the weights chart above). The 2×12 version fixes that, which is what statsmodels does.
ImportantThe argument for the cycle length is called period in current statsmodels and was freq in older versions. Libraries change often, so check the documentation when code from an old notebook fails.

How strong are trend and seasonality?

A useful summary of a decomposition is its strength, a number between 0 and 1:

Ftrend = max(0, 1 − Var(e) / Var(b+e))
Fseason = max(0, 1 − Var(e) / Var(s+e))
def strength(parts):
    d = parts.dropna()
    f_trend  = max(0, 1 - d.resid.var() / (d.trend + d.resid).var())
    f_season = max(0, 1 - d.resid.var() / (d.seasonal + d.resid).var())
    return {"trend strength": round(f_trend, 3), "seasonal strength": round(f_season, 3)}

8 · Change points

Detecting trend changes

A change point is a moment when the behaviour of a series changes: the trend turns upward, the slope gets steeper, or the level jumps. Business causes are easy to name: a new model launch, a competitor entering, a price change or a policy change.

The simulated MobiPlus smartwatch series below has two such changes that the data scientist does not know about yet: a gentle rise, then a steep rise, then a gentle fall.

Way 1: a sliding window

Fit a straight line in a window just before each month and another just after it. If the two slopes differ a lot, something changed there. The score is the absolute difference of the two slopes; it peaks where the trend changes.

Visualizer · sliding-window detector
Smartwatch sales
Slope-change score

def slope_change_score(y, w):
    score = np.full(len(y), np.nan)
    x = np.arange(w)
    for i in range(w, len(y) - w + 1):
        left  = np.polyfit(x, y[i - w:i], 1)[0]
        right = np.polyfit(x, y[i:i + w], 1)[0]
        score[i] = abs(right - left)
    return score

With 𝑊 = 12 the two largest peaks (a minimum gap of one window between them) land at June 2012 and February 2016, against true changes in May 2012 and February 2016: one exact, one a month late. The window size is a trade-off: a short window is jumpy, and a long one blurs close changes.

Way 2: the ruptures library

A change in slope is a change in the average of the month-to-month differences, so the detector runs on the differenced series.

signal = np.diff(watch.values).reshape(-1, 1)
algo = rpt.Binseg(model="l2", min_size=6).fit(signal)
bkps = algo.predict(n_bkps=2)[:-1]       # the last entry is just the series end
[b + 1 for b in bkps]                    # +1 because differencing shortens the series by one

Binary segmentation finds breaks at positions 41 and 86, within one month of the true 40 and 85. It was told how many breaks to look for. When the number is unknown, Pelt with a penalty is used: a bigger penalty means fewer breaks.

Visualizer · penalty explorer
Pelt (model l2, min_size 6) on the differenced smartwatch series.

The penalty controls the answer, from far too many breaks to none at all, so it has to be chosen using domain knowledge, a plot and a test like the one above.

Useful for analysis, not for forecasting

ImportantA change point is only recognised after enough new data has arrived. The sliding window needs 𝑊 months after the change before it can score it. During those months a model is still living in the old regime.
Visualizer · the cost of late detection
Straight line fitted on the first regime (months 0–39) and extended forward.

By the time the change is detected (month 52 with 𝑊 = 12), a forecast that kept extending the old trend is already off by about 350 smartwatches a month. Change point detection explains what happened and when, which tells the business which event moved the trend. It cannot warn about a change before it starts. For forecasting the information is treated as context, for example by fitting the model only on the data after the latest change.

Recap

Key takeaways

  • A moving average smooths by averaging a sliding window: the weighted version favours recent values, the centered version looks both ways and so is for analysis, never for forecasting. Linear interpolation is a centered average.
  • The trend is the slow, non-periodic movement. Estimate it with a long centered rolling average (at least one full cycle) or a regression on time.
  • Seasonality repeats with a fixed, known period. Compute it by removing the trend, averaging each position in the cycle and centering at zero. A series can have more than one.
  • Decomposition splits a series into trend, seasonality and residual. If the residual still has a pattern, decompose it again. If the swing grows with the level, multiply, or add on the log.
  • seasonal_decompose is the same idea in one line. Change points mark where the trend changes, found with a sliding window or ruptures, and they explain the past rather than forecast the future.

Common mistakes

Using a trend window shorter than the cycle, so seasonality leaks into the trend.
Using a centered average anywhere near a forecast.
Calling a residual "fine" because its histogram looks bell-shaped, without checking for leftover cycles.
Using an additive model when the seasonal swing grows with the level.
Using a different period than the data supports, for example 12 on weekly data.
Choosing a change point penalty without looking at the plot.
