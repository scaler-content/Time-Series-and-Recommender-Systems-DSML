Stationarity and Memory - Making a Series Predictable by Removing Structure

Decide whether a series is stationary, make it stationary by differencing, read its memory with the ACF and PACF, avoid the correlation trap, and see why regression on past values is the first model for what is left.

Case: Lanternly (finale)144 months · Jan 2012 – Dec 2023Python · statsmodels
About the dataThis class reuses the Lanternly data from Class 4 and finishes the same story. Units is the series we study, and Complaints (late-delivery complaints per month) is a second series used in Section 8. The 144 months, January 2012 to December 2023, are clean, using the steps from Class 1. The data is simulated for teaching; the numbers are not real figures of any company. The six simulated series in Section 2 use a fixed random seed, so they are the same every time.
1 · Cold open

The average that keeps moving

Last class we gave the Lanternly manager a forecast and a range. Today she comes back with a sharper question: "Your model took out the trend and the season. Is there anything left in what remains? And can I trust a statistic like 'the average month' if it keeps changing?" We first ask whether the average is even a stable number.

Visualizer · rolling mean and spread
12-month rolling mean
12-month rolling standard deviation

The "average month" is 845 units in 2012, 1,607 in 2017 and 2,909 in 2023, and the spread grows from 210 to 391 to 746 units. Both rolling lines climb all the time. There is no single average and no single spread: they depend on when you look.

If the average depends on the date, what does it mean to say that a month is "above average"?
It does not mean much unless we say which period the average is from. Many statistical tools assume that the average and the spread are fixed numbers, so that what we learn from past data still applies to the future. A series that satisfies this is called stationary, and this class is about turning ours into one.

The plan

1 Learn what stationary means, and test it with the ADF test.
2 Remove structure by differencing until the series is stationary.
3 Read the memory of what is left with the ACF and PACF.
4 Avoid the correlation trap.
5 Take the first step towards a model of that memory: regression on lags.
2 · What stationary means

Stationary vs non-stationary: a visual exercise

A series yt is (weakly) stationary if three things do not depend on the time 𝑡:

E[yt] = μ,   Var(yt) = σ²,   Cov(yt, yt+k) = γk depends only on the lag 𝑘.

A stationary series is like a heartbeat on a monitor: it does not matter which minute you look at, the picture looks about the same. A series with a trend, a yearly cycle, or a spread that keeps growing looks different in different minutes, so it is not stationary.

b · trend plus noiseNot stationary: the mean moves from 1.86 to 5.31.

c · yearly waveThe tricky one. The two halves look identical, but the calendar-month means differ by 4.15 units, against 0.8 to 1.2 for a, b, d and f, which is what noise alone produces. A repeating pattern means that the mean depends on 𝑡, so a seasonal series is not stationary.

Categorising a series by eye is subjective. Some people would call f non-stationary because it has bumps. That is why we need a test.

3 · A formal test

The ADF test and its p-value rule

The Augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) test asks one specific question: does the series behave like a random walk? It fits a regression of the change on the previous value:

Δyt = a + γ·yt−1 + Σi=1..p δi·Δyt−i + εt,   Δyt = yt − yt−1
  • The null hypothesis H0 is γ = 0: the series has a unit root (it is a random walk, non-stationary).
  • If γ < 0, a high value tends to be followed by a fall and a low value by a rise, and the series is pulled back to a level: it is stationary.
  • The test reports a p-value. The rule: if the p-value is below 0.05, reject H0 and call the series stationary. Otherwise we cannot.

The null hypothesis is "this is a drunk walker with no home". The test collects evidence that the walker is pulled back home. If the evidence is strong enough, a p-value below 0.05, we say the series has a home: a stationary series.

The t-statistic of the slope on the previous value is the ADF statistic: the same number. γ is only −0.014, so almost no pull back to a level, and the p-value is 0.83, far above 0.05. The library converts the statistic into a p-value using special critical values, because the usual t-table is not valid when the null is a random walk.

def adf_test(series, name="series", show=True):
    pvalue = adfuller(series.dropna())[1]
    verdict = "stationary" if pvalue < 0.05 else "not stationary"
    if show:
        print(f"{name:28s} p-value = {pvalue:.4f}  ->  {verdict}")
    return pvalue
Visualizer · the eye vs the test

The test agrees with the eye on b (p = 0.79), d (p = 0.83), and on the stationary a and f (p near 0). Two results are a surprise: the seasonal series c (p = 0.0068) and the growing-spread series e (p = 0.0000) both pass. ADF only looks for a unit root and a trend-like drift; it does not check for a repeating pattern or a changing spread. Lanternly gets p = 0.996, so it is clearly not stationary, which matches what we saw: trend, season and growing spread.

Important"p below 0.05" does not prove that a series is stationary in the full sense. Use the test together with the plot. A pass on the ADF test means "no unit root", and you still have to look for seasonality and changing variance.
4 · Removing structure

De-trending and de-seasonalising by differencing

To make a series stationary we remove its structure. The simplest tool is differencing: subtract an earlier value.

Removing a trendIf yt = a + b·t + et, then yt − yt−1 = b + (et − et−1): the trend turns into a constant 𝑏 and the rest has a stable mean. It is the discrete version of a derivative, written Δyt or diff(1). A curved trend may need a second difference.
Removing a seasonalityWith a seasonal period of 𝑚, subtract the value from one full cycle ago: yt − yt−m, written diff(m). For monthly data with a yearly cycle, 𝑚 = 12.

To remove a hill from a road profile, record only how much the road goes up or down at each step and ignore the height. To remove the yearly cycle, compare each month only with the same month one year ago.

Visualizer · try each transformation

Each difference costs observations: 1 for diff(1) and 12 more for diff(12), so 13 in total, which is why 131 points are left. The "log" is the multiplicative idea from Class 4 in a new form: the log turns ratios into differences. We keep the last series, called stationary, for the rest of the class. Everything that remains in it is what trend and season could not explain, and its remaining structure is its memory.

log_y = np.log(y)
stationary = log_y.diff().diff(12).dropna()      # 131 points
Can we simply difference until the ADF p-value is below 0.05?
That is a trap. Here diff(1) alone already passes (p = 0.034) but is not clean. Over-differencing is also harmful: it creates a false negative correlation between neighbours and adds noise. Use the minimum number of differences that makes the plot and the test both look stationary, usually not more than 2 in total.

5 · Memory, part one

Autocorrelation and the ACF plot

How much does a series remember its own past? Pair each value with the value 𝑘 steps earlier and compute the correlation. For a stationary series with mean ȳ the autocorrelation at lag 𝑘 is:

rk = Σt=k+1..N (yt − ȳ)(yt−k − ȳ) / Σt=1..N (yt − ȳ)²,   −1 ≤ rk ≤ 1

If I tell you the sales of this month, how much does it tell you about the sales three months ago, or twelve months ago? The autocorrelation is the answer in one number between −1 and 1, for every lag. In code, shift(k) slides the series down by 𝑘 rows (creating 𝑘 missing values at the top, which we skip), and np.corrcoef computes the Pearson correlation.

On a stationary series the difference is small; on a series with a trend the two versions can be very different, which is one more reason to compute the ACF on a stationary series.

6 · Memory, part two

Partial autocorrelation and the PACF plot

The ACF at lag 12 mixes two things: the direct link between yt and yt−12, and the indirect links that pass through the months in between (yt−12 affects yt−11, which affects yt−10, and so on until yt). The partial autocorrelation at lag 𝑘 is the correlation between yt and yt−k after removing the effect of the lags 1, …, 𝑘−1.

Suppose you want to know whether your grandmother's recipe influences your cooking. Your mother learned it from her and you learned it from your mother, so there is a correlation anyway. The partial autocorrelation asks: once I account for what my mother did, is there any extra link left from my grandmother?

To compute it by hand at lag 2: remove the effect of lag 1 from both yt and yt−2 with a regression, then correlate what is left over (the residuals).

X = sm.add_constant(y_lag1)
left_t    = sm.OLS(y_t,    X).fit().resid     # what lag 1 does not explain in y_t
left_lag2 = sm.OLS(y_lag2, X).fit().resid     # what lag 1 does not explain in y_(t-2)
np.corrcoef(left_t, left_lag2)[0, 1]
Visualizer · lag 2, three ways

The partial autocorrelation at lag 2, built from the leftovers, is −0.418, and the library with the same method (method='ols') gives −0.416. The ordinary autocorrelation at lag 2 is only −0.111, so once lag 1 is removed, lag 2 stands out clearly: the indirect path through lag 1 was hiding it. The default method, ywm, gives −0.409, a small difference because it estimates the same quantity in a different way.

Visualizer · PACF explorer

ImportantThe ACF and PACF answer different questions. The ACF shows the total link at each lag, including indirect ones, and is the right tool for finding a seasonal period. The PACF shows only the direct link, and is the tool for deciding how many past values are worth using in a regression. We use the second one in the last section.
7 · What counts as significant

Confidence bands and finding the seasonal period m

The shaded band in the ACF and PACF plots shows what could happen by chance. For a series of 𝑁 points with no memory at all, each autocorrelation is roughly normal with a standard deviation of 1/√𝑁, so about 95% of them fall within:

± 1.96 / √N

A bar that sticks out of the band is called significant.

Visualizer · the band
ACF of pure noise (simulated series a, N = 144), band ±0.163

For pure noise we expect about 2 of 40 bars to cross by chance, and in this sample 1 did, so the band is doing its job. For the stationary Lanternly series (N = 131, band ±0.171), nine lags up to 24 cross the band: 1, 3, 4, 9, 12, 13, 16, 17 and 18. By chance we would expect only about one. Of these, lags 1, 12 and 13 are by far the largest (0.46, 0.48 and 0.39 in size); the others are small bars that only just cross, which is weak evidence. The band printed by statsmodels is slightly wider for higher lags, because the uncertainty of an autocorrelation grows as the lag grows.

ImportantMany bars will cross the band by luck in a long plot. With 95% bands, 1 bar in 20 crosses on pure noise. Focus on the bars that are far outside the band, and on bars at lags that make business sense, such as 1, 12 and 24 for monthly data.

Finding the seasonal period

If we do not know the period, we de-trend the series and look for the highest autocorrelation beyond the first lags. The lag of the strongest peak is the period.

d1 = acf(y.diff().dropna(), nlags=24)
int(np.argmax(d1[2:]) + 2)        # lag of the strongest peak, starting from lag 2
Visualizer · period finder

On Lanternly the strongest peak is at lag 12 (0.81), so 𝑚 = 12, as we assumed from the calendar. On a simulated daily series with a weekly pattern the strongest peak is at lag 7, with 0.86 at both lag 7 and lag 14. The same two lines of code find a period that we did not know in advance. The mystery series is a monthly-like series from a business whose pattern repeats in a different cycle: after removing the slow drift of 0.2 per step with diff(1), its strongest peak is at lag 6 (0.91), so it repeats every 6 steps. If the strongest peak had been at a lag larger than the cycle, we would still check that the multiples of the period (12 and 18 here) are also high.

8 · The correlation trap

Correlation vs causation: confounding and better features

Lanternly also records Complaints, the number of late-delivery complaints per month. The customer-service manager has noticed that complaints and sales move together, and wonders whether complaints could be used to forecast sales. Look at the correlation first.

Visualizer · the shared shadow
Units
Complaints

On the raw series the correlation is 0.983, almost perfect. After a first difference it is still 0.856, and after we remove the trend and the season it is 0.094, close to zero. The strong link was made of two things the series have in common: both grow over the years (the size of the business) and both peak in the festive season (courier load). Complaints do not cause sales, and sales are not caused by complaints. Both are driven by a third factor. This is confounding.

Even if complaints did help to predict sales, could we use them to forecast next November?
No. To forecast November we would need next November's complaints, which we do not have. A feature is only useful if its value is known at the time of the forecast. The test below gives complaints the future values as a favour, so they get the best case.
Visualizer · three regressions on 2023

Three simple regressions of log(units), fitted on the training years (2012–2022) and scored on 2023. Complaints alone, even with the future values supplied, give a MAPE of 5.50%. Time and month of the year, both known in advance, give 4.52%. Adding complaints to those two does not help (4.66%). The features that come from understanding the cause (growth over time, and the festive season) are both better and available when we need them.

ImportantCorrelation is useful for prediction only when the variable is known in advance and the link is stable. Understanding the cause helps us choose better features: a good model for Lanternly uses time and the calendar, and leaves out complaints. The same logic applies to ice cream sales and drownings, which are both driven by temperature, or to cyclists and rain, where the cause runs in the opposite direction.

9 · Using the memory

Introduction to auto regression: regression on lags

The stationary series has memory. The simplest way to use memory is the idea from the regression classes: make the past values the features and the current value the target. This is auto regression, AR(𝑝):

ŷt = α0 + α1·yt−1 + α2·yt−2 + ⋯ + αp·yt−p

The number of lags 𝑝 is a hyperparameter, and the PACF is the tool for choosing it: use the lags up to the last significant partial autocorrelation.

Visualizer · the lag table
First rows of the table for p = 3 (the first three months have no complete history, so they are dropped).

Each row is one month: the target on the left and the three previous months on the right. After this reshaping, the problem is an ordinary regression table, with one condition: the series must be stationary, so that the features are not just a trend in disguise.

Train on 2012 to 2022 (the last 12 months stay unseen) and fit the model for 𝑝 = 1, once with LinearRegression and once with the dedicated AutoReg class:

The R² is 0.216: last month alone explains about 22% of the variation of what is left after removing trend and season.

How many lags are worth using? The PACF said two. Compare 𝑝 = 1, 2, 3 with the AIC from Class 4 (lower is better) and with a one-step-ahead score on the last 12 months, where the lags of the test months are real past values.

Going from one lag to two raises the training R² from 0.216 to 0.354 and lowers the AIC from −318.7 to −336.5. Going to three lags does nothing (R² of 0.343, a worse AIC of −330.8, and a third coefficient of 0.011). That agrees with the PACF, where lag 3 was 0.003: the PACF picks the number of lags, and the AIC confirms it. On the unseen 2023 months, two lags cut the error by about 24% compared with no memory (0.0353 vs 0.0467), and the third lag adds nothing.

ImportantThe regression above works on the stationary series, so its forecasts are changes, not units. To forecast units we must undo the differencing and the log, step by step, which is the job of the ARIMA family. In the next class we add a second kind of memory (past errors), combine both, and let the model do the differencing for us.
Recap

Key takeaways

  • A stationary series has a constant mean, a constant variance, and autocovariances that depend only on the lag. Trend, seasonality and a growing spread each break this.
  • The ADF test has the null hypothesis of a unit root. A p-value below 0.05 means stationary. It does not detect seasonality or a changing spread, so always look at the plot as well.
  • Differencing removes structure: diff(1) removes a trend and diff(m) removes a seasonal cycle. Take the logarithm first when the swing grows with the level. Use the minimum number of differences.
  • The ACF gives the correlation with each lag and is the tool for finding the seasonal period. The PACF removes the indirect links and is the tool for choosing the number of past values. The band is ±1.96/√N.
  • Correlation is not causation. Series that share a trend or a season correlate strongly, and the link disappears once those are removed. Prefer features that are caused by something real and known in advance.
  • Auto regression is linear regression on past values, AR(𝑝), and the PACF picks 𝑝.

Common mistakes

Calling a series stationary because the ADF p-value is small, without looking at seasonality and spread.
Differencing more than needed, which creates a false negative correlation.
Computing the ACF of a series with a trend and reading the slow decay as memory.
Treating every bar that crosses the band as meaningful.
Using a correlated variable that will not be known at forecast time.
Fitting an AR model on a non-stationary series.
Closing the Lanternly storyThe manager's question has an answer. Lanternly's units are not stationary: they have a trend, and a yearly cycle with a swing that grows with the size of the business. After a log, one ordinary difference and one seasonal difference (12 months), the series passes the ADF test with a steady spread. What is left is not empty: it carries a negative memory at lags 1 and 2 and a yearly memory at lags 12 and 13, which a regression on past values can already use. The complaints series looked like a great predictor and was only a shadow of the same growth and festive season, so Lanternly will forecast with time and the calendar. That closes the story.
