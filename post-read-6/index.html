The ARIMA Family Reunion: AR, MA, ARIMA and SARIMA Without the Guesswork

Build the ARIMA family step by step, from AR and MA to SARIMA, choose the orders with evidence instead of guesswork, check the residuals properly, and compare the final models with rolling-origin cross-validation.

About the dataThis class starts a new story with a new file. VoltNest sells batteries for home inverters: 168 months, January 2011 to December 2024, no missing values, already clean using the steps from Class 1. 1 · Cold open

Two analysts, two winners

VoltNest sells home inverter batteries, and sales jump every summer when power cuts begin. The planning head must order stock twelve months ahead and wants a forecast error under 5% MAPE. She has two analysts. The first swears by Holt-Winters, which we built earlier. The second swears by a model called SARIMA. Each has brought a slide that proves their model is better, and both slides use real numbers. How is that possible?

Visualizer · the series

The series climbs over the years and has a tall peak every summer, so it has both a trend and a yearly seasonality. Between the peaks, the level also wanders up and down in a way that a straight line does not explain.

Each analyst trains on all the data before a year and forecasts the twelve months of that year. Treat the SARIMA line as a black box for now; by the end of the class every number inside it will make sense.

Both slides are true. On 2021, Holt-Winters has a MAPE of 5.42% and SARIMA has 7.33%, so Analyst 1 wins. On 2023, Holt-Winters has 7.70% and SARIMA has 5.96%, so Analyst 2 wins. The winner depends on which year you test on. One test period is one exam paper, and a student can be lucky on one paper.

Which model would you pick for next year, and what extra evidence would you want?
A good answer is "neither yet". We want the same contest repeated over several periods, we want to know that each model is built correctly, and we want to check that its errors have no pattern left.

The plan

1 Build the family one member at a time: AR, MA, ARMA, ARIMA, SARIMA.
2 Choose the orders with the ACF and PACF, grid search and auto_arima.
3 Check the residuals.
4 Settle the contest with rolling-origin cross-validation.
2 · Memory of past values

Auto regression: lags as features, PACF and SES weights

The smoothing models describe a series through its level, trend and season. But what about a stationary series, which has none of these? It looks random, yet there can still be information left in it: Class 5 showed that this month's value can be related to earlier values.

Suppose our series came with an extra column that helps to explain it. We could simply run a linear regression. A univariate series has no extra column, so we create features from the series itself: the value at time 𝑡 is the target, and the values at 𝑡−1, 𝑡−2, …, 𝑡−𝑝 are the features.

ŷt = α0 + α1yt−1 + α2yt−2 + ⋯ + αpyt−p,   yt = ŷt + εt
SymbolMeaning
α0intercept (parameter)
α1, …, αpweights on the past values (parameters, learnt from data)
εterror term, what the past values cannot explain
pnumber of past values used (hyperparameter, chosen by us)

This is auto regression of order p, written AR(p). "Auto" means that the series is regressed on itself. If I want to guess today's temperature and I have no weather station, I still have one very good source: the last few days. Auto regression is exactly that: the past values of the series become the columns of an ordinary regression table.

AR needs a stationary series, so we set the test year aside and check the training data first.

After one difference the series train_st holds month-to-month changes, not units. Important: as in Class 5, the ADF test does not look for seasonality. train_st still contains the yearly cycle; we keep this in mind and see what it costs.

Visualizer · the lag table

The first three columns are the past values y(t−1), y(t−2), y(t−3) and the last column is the value to predict. A value that is the target in one month becomes y(t−1) in the next month, then y(t−2), then y(t−3). The first three monthly changes have no complete history, so they are dropped.

When does AR make sense, and what is p?

AR is useful when the past has a direct link with the present. The partial autocorrelation (PACF) measures that: the correlation with lag 𝑘 after removing what lags 1 to 𝑘−1 already explain. A feature that only repeats what the other features already say adds little to a regression, and the PACF helps us to avoid it.

Visualizer · PACF of the monthly changes

The PACF is 0.45 at lag 1 and −0.50 at lag 2, both far outside the band of ±0.157. At lag 3 it drops to about −0.13, inside the band. So the first reading is p = 2: two past values carry the short-range memory. A negative bar counts just as much as a positive one; the size matters. There are large bars again further to the right (around lags 8 to 12), which come from the seasonality that we did not remove.

Fit AR(2) as a plain linear regression: the intercept is 4.38, α1 = 0.654, α2 = −0.489, and R² on the training data is 0.388. The last two monthly changes explain about 39% of the variation in this month's change. If last month rose, this month tends to rise too, but a rise two months ago pulls it back.

How is this different from Simple Exponential Smoothing?

SES from Class 3 also takes a weighted average of past values, and it even looks like a regression:

ŷt+1 = α·yt + α(1−α)·yt−1 + α(1−α)²·yt−2 + …
Visualizer · weights on past values

The SES weights (0.5, 0.25, 0.125, … at α = 0.5) are always positive and always shrinking, all fixed by one number. The AR(6) weights are learnt from the data one by one: positive at lag 1, negative at lag 2, with no required shape.

QuestionSESAR(p)
Shape of the weightsexponentially decayingany shape, learnt from data
Hyperparameterαp

SES is a recipe with one dial. Turn it, and all the weights move together. AR gives you one dial for every past value and lets the data set each of them.

3 · AR in code

Forecast the changes, then integrate

For the rest of the class we use one function, SARIMAX, for every model in the family. It takes an order tuple of three numbers, (p, d, q): the first is the AR order 𝑝, the second is the number of differences 𝑑, the third is the MA order 𝑞. For a pure AR model we set the last two to zero. We start with the simplest one, (1, 0, 0), on the differenced series.

model = SARIMAX(train_st, order=(1, 0, 0)).fit(disp=False)
changes = model.forecast(steps=12)

The AR(1) coefficient is 0.446. The forecasts are monthly changes, and they die out quickly: −31.2, −13.9, −6.2 and then almost zero. This is how AR behaves: each forecast is a fraction (here 0.446) of the previous one, so the memory fades away. To get units back we integrate: add up the forecast changes and start from the last known value.

ŷT+h = yT + Σj=1..h ΔŷT+j

If I know your bank balance today and the amount that goes in or out every month, I can rebuild the balance for every future month: a cumulative sum plus the last value does exactly that.

The AR(1) MAPE is 16.78%, far above the target of 5%. The forecast is almost a flat line because the forecast changes shrink to zero, while the real 2024 has a large summer peak. AR(2) cuts the MAPE from 16.78% to 10.56%. Its coefficients, 0.665 and −0.491, are almost the same as our own regression (0.654 and −0.489); the small gap comes from the estimation method (SARIMAX uses maximum likelihood) and from the rows used, because our table dropped the first three months. AR(3) gives 11.19%, slightly worse: its third coefficient is only −0.136, which agrees with the small PACF bar at lag 3.

The clean rule is: if the ACF decays gradually and the PACF cuts off after lag 𝑘, an AR(𝑘) model is a candidate. Here the ACF has large bars far out (lag 12 is the biggest) because the seasonality is still inside, so the plot does not give a perfectly clean answer. This is where a human judgement call is needed: we read the first few lags for the short memory and treat the later bars as leftover season.

4 · Memory of surprises

Moving average models: memory of past surprises

AR uses past values as features. What if we use past errors instead? Every month differs from what we expected, and that surprise is a new number that is unique to each point, so it can be a feature too. This is the moving average model.

ImportantThis has nothing to do with moving average smoothing from Class 2. Only the name is the same. There we averaged past values; here we regress on past forecast errors.
ŷt = μ + m1et−1,   et−1 = yt−1 − ŷt−1
ŷt = μ + m1et−1 + m2et−2 + ⋯ + mqet−q,   yt = ŷt + εt
SymbolMeaning
μmean level of the (stationary) series
et−1the error, or surprise, at time 𝑡−1: actual minus forecast
m1, …, mqconstants learnt from data
εtthe part that is truly random and cannot be helped
qthe hyperparameter of the MA(q) model

A first intuition is "how far was last month from the average". The model sharpens this: the error is measured from its own forecast for that month, so errors are computed one after another, from the start of the series. AR remembers what happened. MA remembers how surprised we were. A shop that sold far more than planned last week adjusts this week's plan because of the surprise, and after a few weeks the surprise is forgotten.

The textbook signatures

Before using real data, look at two simulated series whose true type we know: an AR(2) (yt = 0.5yt−1 + 0.3yt−2 + et) and an MA(2) (yt = et + 0.7et−1 + 0.5et−2), each with 500 points after the warm-up.

5 · Putting the two together

ARMA, stationarity and ARIMA

ARMA: both memories together

Combine the two ideas: past values and past errors. This is ARMA(p, q), where 𝑝 is the AR order and 𝑞 is the MA order. They do not have to be equal. In code, 𝑝 and 𝑞 are the first and third numbers of order.

ŷt = c + α1yt−1 + ⋯ + αpyt−p + m1et−1 + ⋯ + mqet−q
yt = c + Σi=1..p αiyt−i + Σj=1..q mjet−j + εt

Why does ARMA need a stationary series?

ARMA learns one mean and one set of weights and assumes that they hold at all times. Its long-range forecast always returns to that mean. If the level of the series keeps moving, there is no such mean. See what happens when we ignore this and fit ARMA(2, 2) with a constant on the raw units.

The forecast slides from 3,634 units down to about 3,154 after a year and then stays near 3,129 for good, far below the recent level of the business. It drifts back towards an old average, and the MAPE on 2024 is the worst so far. This is why the series must be stationary before ARMA is applied.

Now use ARMA correctly, on the changes: ARMA(3, 3) reaches 7.25%, a little better than the best pure model, MA(3) at 7.35%. We differenced by hand before the model and integrated by hand after it.

ARIMA: let the model do the differencing

When the series is not stationary, the recipe is: (1) difference the series 𝑑 times to remove the trend, (2) fit ARMA(p, q) to the result, (3) integrate the forecasts 𝑑 times to bring the trend back. This is ARIMA(p, d, q), where the I stands for "integrated":

ARIMA(p, d, q) = Integrate d times [ AR(p) + MA(q) on the series differenced d times ]

Use 𝑑 = 1 for a roughly linear trend and 𝑑 = 2 for a curved (quadratic) trend. With 𝑑 = 2, integrating by hand means two cumulative sums, each with its own starting value, which is easy to get wrong. Passing 𝑑 in order lets the library do it. Differencing is taking off your shoes at the door, ARMA is the work done inside, and integration is putting the shoes back on; ARIMA does all three, so you cannot forget your shoes.

for order in [(0, 1, 1), (3, 1, 3), (3, 2, 3)]:
    fit = SARIMAX(train, order=order).fit(disp=False)    # raw units go in, no manual cumsum
    forecast = fit.forecast(steps=12)

6 · Seasonal lags

SARIMA: seasonal lags, P, D, Q and s

To handle seasonality with ARIMA we would have to difference at the seasonal period (yt − yt−s) to remove the season, fit AR and MA terms, and add the season back to the forecasts. Try it by hand once, and compare with the library.

seasonal_change = train.diff(12).dropna()                           # step 1: remove the season
inner = SARIMAX(seasonal_change, order=(2, 1, 1)).fit(disp=False)    # step 2: ARIMA on what is left
by_hand = inner.forecast(12) + train[-12:].values                    # step 3: add last year's values back
library = SARIMAX(train, order=(2, 1, 1), seasonal_order=(0, 1, 0, 12)).fit(disp=False).forecast(12)

The season length s

We find 𝑠 from the ACF of the de-trended series, as in Class 5. A second option is to treat 𝑠 as a hyperparameter and compare a few values on a validation year (2023), keeping 2024 out of the choice.

Visualizer · validating s on 2023

The ACF of the changes at lags 6, 12 and 24 is −0.04, 0.85 and 0.78, so 𝑠 = 12. The validation run agrees: 𝑠 = 12 has the lowest MAPE on 2023 (5.96%, against 6.21% or more for the others) and a clearly lower RMSE. Reading the ACF is the sensible first step, and the loop is a useful check.

How seasonal lags work

For a normal AR term, order 2 means lags 𝑡−1 and 𝑡−2: in March 2024, that is February 2024 and January 2024. For a seasonal term, order 2 means lags 𝑡−𝑠 and 𝑡−2𝑠: in March 2024, that is March 2023 and March 2022.

  • P is an auto regression on the same month in earlier years: Σ βiyt−is
  • Q is a moving average on the errors of the same month in earlier years: Σ ciet−is
  • D is seasonal differencing: for D = 1, y′t = yt − yt−12 (compare d = 1: y′t = yt − yt−1)

The small letters ask "what happened in the last few months". The capital letters ask "what happened in this same month in the last few years".

7 · Evidence, not guesswork

Choosing orders: ACF/PACF guide, grid search and overfitting

We now have seven numbers to choose. Work in this order: (1) 𝑠 from the calendar and the ACF; (2) 𝑑 and 𝐷: the fewest differences that make the series look stationary; (3) 𝑝, 𝑞, 𝑃, 𝑄 from the ACF and PACF, then confirm with a search.

For VoltNest, 𝑠 = 12, and we take 𝑑 = 1 and 𝐷 = 1. (The summer swing stays about the same size over the years. If it grew with the level, we would take the log first, as in Class 5.) Plot the doubly differenced series.

Grid search

A grid search fits every combination and ranks them by the AIC from Class 4, which rewards a good fit and charges for every parameter (lower is better). We keep 𝑑 = 1, 𝐷 = 1 fixed and try 𝑝, 𝑞 ∈ {0, 1, 2} and 𝑃, 𝑄 ∈ {0, 1}: 36 models.

ImportantAIC values are comparable only between models fitted to the same differenced data. Never use AIC to compare different values of 𝑑 or 𝐷.
Visualizer · the 36-model grid

The lowest AIC, 1648.8, belongs to SARIMA(2,1,1)(0,1,1,12), with 5 parameters (2 AR, 1 MA, 1 seasonal MA and the noise variance). It agrees with the plot reading of Q = 1 and P = 0. The next four models are within about 2 to 5 AIC points, and most of them only add a term to it. Two of the 36 models did not converge, which is why the table has that column. The grid used only the training data.

Overfitting: why not use large p and q?

More parameters can always fit the training data a little better. Increase 𝑝 = 𝑞 = 𝑘, train up to 2022, and score on 2023.

Visualizer · more terms, more noise

The training RMSE falls every time, from 83.1 to 69.6 units, so it can never tell us to stop. The 2023 error goes from 7.73% at k = 0 to its best value of 5.69% at k = 3 (not in a straight line, since k = 1 is at 7.99%), then rises again to 5.76% and 6.20%. Past that point the extra weights are fitting noise: overfitting. BIC is lowest at k = 2 and AIC at k = 4; both charge for parameters, BIC charges more.

A student who memorises last year's paper gets full marks on last year's paper. Large p and q do the same with last year's data.

8 · Automatic search

Automatic order search with auto_arima

auto_arima from the pmdarima library automates the three steps: it chooses 𝑑 with a statistical test (KPSS by default) and 𝐷 with a seasonal test (OCSB by default); it searches over 𝑝, 𝑞, 𝑃, 𝑄 (with stepwise=True it starts from a few models and moves to neighbours that lower the AIC, so it does not fit every combination); and it returns the best model found.

pm.arima.ndiffs(train, test="kpss")            # d
pm.arima.nsdiffs(train, m=12, test="ocsb")     # D
auto = pm.auto_arima(train, seasonal=True, m=12, stepwise=True, trace=True,
                     suppress_warnings=True, error_action="ignore")

The stepwise trace tried about 24 candidates; several show AIC = inf, which means they were rejected, for example because the fit was unstable.

Three things deserve attention:

  • It chose D = 0. The seasonal test asked for no seasonal difference, so the search handled the season with seasonal AR and MA terms. Our grid used D = 1.
  • Its AIC of 1831.3 cannot be compared with the grid's 1648.8, because the two models are fitted to differently differenced data. A lower or higher number here means nothing.
  • Lines with AIC = inf in the trace are candidates that were rejected.
If auto_arima has the best 2024 score, are we done?
No. 2024 is one year, and the cold open showed what one year can do. auto_arima is a fast way to get a good candidate. Its result still needs the residual checks and the repeated contest that follow.
9 · Is anything left?

Residual diagnostics

The residuals are actual minus fitted values. A good model has taken all the structure out, so the residuals should look like random noise:

PropertyWhy it mattersCheck
Uncorrelateda pattern left over is information the model missedACF of residuals, Ljung-Box test
Zero meanotherwise the forecasts are biased high or lowmean, t-test
Normalthe usual forecast intervals assume ithistogram, Q-Q plot, Jarque-Bera test

The Ljung-Box statistic adds up the squared autocorrelations rk of the residuals up to lag h. Its null hypothesis is "no autocorrelation". In all three tests, a p-value above 0.05 is the result we hope for.

QLB = n(n+2) Σk=1..h rk² / (n−k)

After squeezing an orange, you check the peel. If juice still drips out, you did not squeeze well. The residuals are the peel.

A trap: the start-up residuals

Visualizer · the first residuals

The first residual is 2,096, the whole sales figure of the first month: the model has no history yet. With d = 1 and D = 1, the first 1 + 12 = 13 residuals are start-up values. If we keep them, the normality test fails completely (p = 0.0000). Without them it passes (p = 0.20). Always drop the first d + D×s residuals.

Visualizer · a model that passes, and models that fail

The grid winner passes all checks: zero mean (4.97 units against a standard deviation of 73.2, t-test p = 0.419), uncorrelated (Ljung-Box p-values of 0.589 and 0.363; the stricter version, which allows for the 4 fitted weights, still passes with 0.244 and 0.172), and normal (Jarque-Bera p = 0.20). 10 · A fair contest

Rolling-origin cross-validation

Here are all the models of the class, scored on 2024.

Visualizer · the leaderboard on 2024

The seasonal models are far ahead of the rest, between about 2% and 3%. Their order among themselves rests on one year that we have looked at many times, so it is not a fair way to pick between them.

Rolling-origin cross-validation repeats the real forecasting task several times. Pick a point in the past (the origin), train on everything before it, forecast the next ℎ months, record the error, move the origin forward and repeat. The final score is the average:

CV score = (1/K) Σk=1..K MAPE( yTk+1 : Tk+h, ŷTk+1 : Tk+h )

where Tk is the last training month of fold 𝑘. The model never sees the future of its own fold. Shuffled k-fold is not allowed, for the reason we saw in Class 3: it trains on the future to predict the past. Instead of one exam, we give each model five exams from five different years, and in every exam it may study only what came before.

We keep 2024 aside and make five folds inside 2011 to 2023, each testing one year. TimeSeriesSplit builds them.

Visualizer · the five folds

Five folds test 2019, 2020, 2021, 2022 and 2023. The training window expands from 96 to 144 months. (A sliding window, which drops old months and keeps a fixed length, is the alternative when old behaviour no longer applies.)

def rolling_origin(forecaster):
    errors = {}
    for tr_idx, te_idx in folds:
        history, actual = development.iloc[tr_idx], development.iloc[te_idx]
        predicted = np.asarray(forecaster(history))
        errors[actual.index[0].year] = np.mean(np.abs(actual.values - predicted) / actual.values) * 100
    return pd.Series(errors)
Visualizer · the bar to beat

The seasonal naive baseline from Class 3 ranges from 2.24% in 2023 to 9.69% in 2021. The same simple recipe looks excellent or poor depending on the year, which is why we average over folds. Every serious model must beat this bar.

Recap

Key takeaways

  • AR(p) is a regression on the last 𝑝 values. MA(q) is a regression on the last 𝑞 forecast errors, and it is unrelated to moving average smoothing.
  • AR weights are learnt freely; SES weights decay exponentially from one α.
  • PACF cut-off suggests 𝑝; ACF cut-off suggests 𝑞. When both decay, search.
  • ARMA needs a stationary series. ARIMA differences 𝑑 times, fits ARMA and integrates back.
  • SARIMA adds (P, D, Q)s, which look back whole seasons. It handles one season length.
  • Choose 𝑑 and 𝐷 first; compare AIC only between models with the same differencing.
  • Large 𝑝 and 𝑞 always lower the training error and can raise the forecast error.
  • auto_arima gives a fast candidate, not a final answer.
  • Good residuals are uncorrelated, zero mean and normal; drop the start-up residuals first.
  • Compare models with rolling-origin cross-validation, and prefer the simpler model when the gap is small.

Common mistakes

Fitting AR, MA or ARMA to a series that is not stationary.
Forgetting to integrate the forecasts of a differenced series.
Ignoring the convergence flag because warnings are hidden.
Comparing AIC values across different 𝑑 or 𝐷.
Testing the residuals with the start-up values included.
Picking a winner from one test year, or from a test year that was used for tuning.
