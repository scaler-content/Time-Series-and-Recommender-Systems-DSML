Beat the Baseline - Metrics, Honest Splits and the Simple Forecasts That Win

How to measure a forecast fairly, split time series data without cheating, and see how far the simplest forecasting methods go, because every clever model must beat them.

1 · Cold open

The vendor with the 9% claim

You are the data scientist at ChillPoint, a chain of ice cream parlours. Every month ChillPoint orders milk, cones and freezer space weeks in advance, so it needs a forecast of tubs sold. A software vendor walks in: "Our AI forecasting tool has a 9% error. It costs a lot, but it is clearly excellent." The manager turns to you: "Is 9% good?"

You cannot answer yet, and that is the point of this class. A number like 9% means nothing until it is compared with something. Look at the history first.

2 · Yardsticks

Forecast metrics: MAE, RMSE and MAPE

A forecast error is the actual value minus the forecast: et = yt − ŷt. To judge a whole forecast, the errors are combined into one number.

MetricFormulaUnitBest used when
MAE(1/N) Σ |et|Same as the dataYou want the typical size of a miss, in tubs
RMSE√( (1/N) Σ et² )Same as the dataBig misses hurt much more than small ones
MAPE(1/N) Σ |et / yt|PercentYou need a scale-free number for management, such as "within 5%"

MAE is the average distance between where the dart landed and the bullseye. RMSE squares each distance first, so one wild throw counts much more than several small misses.

Visualizer · metric lab
Four months, actual = 100 each
Set the four forecasts yourself, or load one of the two from the class. Forecast A makes four small misses of 10; forecast B makes three perfect forecasts and one miss of 40.

MAE gives both preset forecasts a score of 10 and cannot tell them apart. RMSE gives 10 to A and 20 to B, because squaring makes the single miss of 40 count much more. If a big miss means an empty freezer on a hot day, RMSE is the metric that reflects the cost.

Why MAPE is scale-free

Visualizer · same miss, different size

A miss of 10 is 10% of 100 but only 1% of 1,000. MAPE makes errors comparable across series of different sizes, which is why management likes it, and it is the metric behind requirements such as "not more than 5%". Its weak spot: if an actual value is zero, the division is undefined (see the last section).

def performance(actual, predicted, show=True):
    scores = {"MAE": mae(actual, predicted),
              "RMSE": mse(actual, predicted) ** 0.5,
              "MAPE %": mape(actual, predicted) * 100}
    ...
    return scores

One helper scores every method, so each is judged the same way. On forecast B it returns MAE 10.0, RMSE 20.0 and MAPE 10.00% (the miss of 40 spread over four rows of 100).

Which metric would you show the CFO, and which would you use to choose between two models?
MAPE is easy for a CFO to read as a percentage. For a model comparison, use the metric that matches the business cost, and report more than one.

3 · Honest tests

Honest splits for time series

A fair test means judging a forecast on data it never saw. For tabular problems that is train_test_split, which shuffles rows. For a time series, shuffling is cheating: if July 2017 lands in the test set and July 2018 in the training set, the model has already seen what happened after the month it is asked to predict.

Visualizer · random vs time split
All 120 months, one row per year. The test set holds 12 months either way. The random split uses the class's seed (default_rng(0)).
traintest

# Split 1: random shuffle, 108 rows to train and 12 to test
order = np.random.default_rng(0).permutation(len(ice))
train_idx, test_idx = order[:108], order[108:]
# Split 2: honest time split
rf2 = RandomForestRegressor(n_estimators=200, random_state=0).fit(X.iloc[:108], ice.iloc[:108])

The model is a random forest learning from the month number 𝑡 and the calendar month, easy to fool. The same model on the same data looks clearly better under the random split: MAPE 3.8% with the shuffle against 5.8% on the honest split. With shuffling every test month sits between training months, so the forest just recalls its neighbours. In reality we must predict a year after everything we know, and a random forest cannot continue a rising trend beyond the largest 𝑡 it has seen. The random split hid that weakness.

Split by time

Keep the last 12 months for the test set so the test is exactly the forecasting job of a real year: nine years (Jan 2009 – Dec 2017, 108 months) to train, the final year (Jan – Dec 2018, 12 months) to test. There is only one column of data, so no separate y_train: the forecast is built from the past values of the series.

train = ice.loc[:"2017-12-01"]
test  = ice.loc["2018-01-01":]

More than one test: walking forward

One test year can be lucky or unlucky. TimeSeriesSplit makes several train/test pairs, and in each the test always comes after the training data. Each fold gives a score, and the average is a more stable verdict.

Visualizer · walk-forward folds

Shuffled K-fold has the same leakage problem as the shuffled split, so avoid it for time series. In this class the single last-year test is used for simplicity.

4 · Baselines

Simple forecasts: mean, median, naive, seasonal naive and drift

Four rules need no model at all. Each forecasts all 12 test months from the training data only. Switch them on and off, and watch the scoreboard.

Visualizer · forecast explorer
History 2016–2017, actual 2018 and every forecast made at the end of 2017. Hover a month for values.

Scores on the 2018 test year. Highlighted rows are the forecasts currently drawn.

1 · Mean and medianPredict the average (or median) of all history for every future month. Flat: the next point barely changes the mean, so the forecast responds to nothing. The mean forecast is a single number, about 4,707 tubs, for every month; it ignores the trend and the cycle and misses badly at the summer peak. A baseline to beat, not a useful forecast.
2 · NaiveForecast every future month with the last observed value. Here that is December 2017, a low month, so summer is badly under-forecast. The value is made once from the training data and used for all 12 months. Re-forecasting every month would change the answer for all later months: a client told in March that June will be X, and in April that it will be Y, loses trust. Only a benchmark.
3 · Seasonal naiveUse the same month last year: the forecast for July 2018 is the actual of July 2017. This is the sentence kept from the cold open. It respects the cycle and copies a full year of shape, but cannot add growth, so it tends to sit below the actuals when the series is rising.
4 · DriftDraw a straight line between the first and last training point and extend it. The slope is the average change per month (19.9 tubs per month here). It adds the growth that seasonal naive misses, but starts from low-season December and has no cycle.
ŷT+h = yT + h · (yT − y1) / (T − 1)
forecasts["Seasonal naive"] = pd.Series(train.iloc[-12:].values, index=test.index)
slope = (train.iloc[-1] - train.iloc[0]) / (T - 1)
forecasts["Drift"] = pd.Series(train.iloc[-1] + slope * h, index=test.index)

Drift is fragile

Visualizer · move drift's anchor

Changing the end point by a single month moves the MAPE from 21.2% to 19.1%, because the line is anchored to one point. The error stays high either way (about 20%); only seasonal naive follows the shape of the year.

Which rule would you combine to get both the shape and the growth?
Seasonal naive plus drift: the cycle of last year, lifted by the yearly growth. A short step toward the smoothing models that come next.
Why study rules this simple?
Sometimes one of them is the best method available, and in many cases they are the benchmark. A new model is only worth considering if it beats the simple alternatives.
5 · Moving average

The moving average forecast and its flat-line problem

In Class 2 the moving average was a smoothing tool. As a forecast, we average the last 𝑚 values for the next month. For the second future month we average the last 𝑚 values again, now including the first forecast, and so on: the forecast becomes part of the history.

def ma_forecast(history, window, steps, index):
    values = list(history.values)
    out = []
    for _ in range(steps):
        nxt = np.mean(values[-window:])
        out.append(nxt)
        values.append(nxt)             # the forecast becomes part of the history
    return pd.Series(out, index=index)
Visualizer · forecasts feeding forecasts

Both the window of 3 and the window of 12 flatten out within a few steps, and the first values already ignore the shape of the year. The window of 3 settles near the level of the last quarter of 2017, a low-season level. The window of 12 averages the whole last year, so it lands at the yearly level, which is better, but still a flat line without any seasonal shape. The reason is the recursion: from the second forecast onwards the window is filled with our own forecasts, so it averages averages.

The method also uses very little of the nine years of history: only the last 𝑚 points matter. We want to keep a memory of the whole series and still give the most weight to recent months. That is the next idea.

6 · Exponential smoothing

Simple exponential smoothing

Remember everything, weigh the recent most

Simple exponential smoothing (SES) gives the newest value a weight 𝛼 and older values smaller and smaller weights that decay exponentially. 𝛼 is the smoothing level, between 0 and 1.

ŷt+1 = α·yt + (1 − α)·ŷt
weights: α, α(1−α), α(1−α)², …
Visualizer · SES lab
Weight given to each past month
Forecasts of 2018

A high 𝛼 puts nearly all the weight on the last month, so the forecast reacts quickly and forgets history. A low 𝛼 spreads the weight over many months, so the forecast is steady and slow to react. The statsmodels SimpleExpSmoothing can be checked by writing the update loop yourself:

level = train.iloc[0]
for y in train:
    level = alpha * y + (1 - alpha) * level
# our loop: 5327.2826     statsmodels 1st forecast: 5327.2826

The numbers are identical, so there is no mystery in the library: SES is a running weighted average with one number to choose.

Multi-step versus one-step

12 steps in one goFor horizons beyond the first step the model has no next actual value, so it keeps reusing its own latest level: a single repeated value. SES has no trend and no seasonality. MAPE 15.4%.
One step at a timeEach month, after the actual arrives, the level is updated and only the next month is forecast. With α=0.3, MAPE 15.2%: barely better, because the level reacts too slowly to a strongly seasonal series.

The two scores answer different business questions. ChillPoint orders weeks ahead, so the honest score for it is the multi-step one.

def ses_one_step(history, future, alpha):
    level = history.iloc[0]
    for y in history:
        level = alpha * y + (1 - alpha) * level
    out = []
    for y in future:
        out.append(level)                          # forecast for this month, made one month earlier
        level = alpha * y + (1 - alpha) * level    # now the actual arrives
    return pd.Series(out, index=future.index)

Choosing α

Visualizer · α grid
One-step MAPE on the 2018 test year for five values of α (computed from the training data's final level).

A rule of thumb for monthly data is a starting value of 12×12, about 0.04. Here the best α is much higher: the cycle makes the series swing from month to month, so the level has to chase the data. The estimate from statsmodels on the training data is 1.0 to three decimal places, and with α=1 the update becomes ŷt+1 = yt, the naive forecast. That is a warning sign: SES could not find any better use of the history, since it has no seasonality. Even at α=0.9 the one-step MAPE is about 9.8%, still behind the 6.3% of seasonal naive. The estimate uses the training data only, which is the honest way; picking α by looking at the test score would be a quiet leak.

8 · Awkward data

MASE and sMAPE when actuals are near zero

MAPE divides by the actual value, which causes two problems: it explodes when actuals are small and it is undefined at exactly zero. ChillPoint's hot chocolate kiosk shows this clearly.

Visualizer · the hot chocolate kiosk

With seasonal naive and a 12-month moving average as the two forecasts, the test-year actuals are [25 27 22 9 6 0 0 1 5 12 20 29]. When the actual is 0, MAPE divides by zero; scikit-learn avoids a crash by swapping in a tiny number, so the moving-average forecast, which is not exactly zero in those months, gets a MAPE of about 9e17 percent: a number to never show a manager. Seasonal naive matches the zeros exactly, but its MAPE (43.4%) is still inflated by the months with 1 and 5 cups. MAE tells the honest story in plain units: a typical miss of a few cups.

Two alternatives

sMAPESymmetric MAPE divides the miss by the average size of the actual and the forecast, so a single zero actual no longer breaks it.
sMAPE = (100/N) Σ 2|yt − ŷt| / (|yt| + |ŷt|)
MASECompares the forecast's MAE with the MAE of a naive forecast on the training data. Below 1 means better than the naive baseline, above 1 worse. It never divides by the actual, so zeros are harmless.
MASE = MAE on test set ÷ MAE of in-sample (seasonal) naive on train

MASE answers the question asked all class, "is this better than the simple rule?", in a single number that works on any series, even one with zeros.

def smape(actual, predicted):
    actual, predicted = np.asarray(actual, float), np.asarray(predicted, float)
    denom = np.abs(actual) + np.abs(predicted)
    ratio = np.divide(2 * np.abs(actual - predicted), denom, out=np.zeros_like(denom), where=denom != 0)
    return ratio.mean() * 100

def mase(actual, predicted, train, m=1):
    scale = np.mean(np.abs(train.values[m:] - train.values[:-m]))   # in-sample naive error, season length m
    return np.mean(np.abs(np.asarray(actual) - np.asarray(predicted))) / scale
Visualizer · metrics on the kiosk
Same two forecasts, four metrics. MAPE blows up; sMAPE and MASE stay finite.

The MASE of seasonal naive is 1.04, about 1 by construction: the denominator is the error the same copy-last-year rule made on the training years, so the rule is about as accurate on the test year as before. The moving-average forecast scores about 2, twice as bad as the baseline, a verdict no zero in the data can break. One caution: sMAPE is not truly symmetric (it punishes under- and over-forecasts a little differently), so use it alongside MAE, not instead of it.

sMAPE in a corner case

Visualizer · sMAPE calculator 
def smape_one(actual, predicted):
    if actual == 0 and predicted == 0:
        return 0.0                              # no error, and no division by zero
    return 100 * 2 * abs(actual - predicted) / (abs(actual) + abs(predicted))

A forecast of 5 against an actual of 0 scores the maximum, 200%, and a perfect zero forecast scores 0. Both are finite and sensible, which plain MAPE could not manage. Always decide and state how zero over zero is treated.

Recap

Key takeaways

  • MAE is the typical miss in the data's units, RMSE punishes large misses, and MAPE is a scale-free percentage. Report more than one, and match the metric to the business cost.
  • Never shuffle a time series. Split by time and test on the final period, or use walk-forward folds that always test after the training data.
  • The simple forecasts are mean, median, naive, seasonal naive and drift. On a seasonal series, seasonal naive is the one to beat.
  • A moving average forecast turns flat because forecasts feed forecasts. SES keeps the whole history with decaying weights set by α, but is flat for 12 steps without new actuals. One-step and multi-step scores answer different questions.
  • A baseline is the bar: a model must clearly beat the best simple rule. MASE and sMAPE work when actuals are near zero, and MASE directly says whether the model beats the naive rule.

Common mistakes

Using train_test_split with shuffling on a time series.
Tuning a parameter such as α on the test year.
Quoting a MAPE for a series that contains zeros.
Comparing a one-step score with a 12-step score as if they were the same task.
Judging a model by its error alone, without a baseline score next to it.
Using a seasonal naive forecast with the wrong season length for the data frequency.
Closing the ChillPoint storyThe manager gets a plain answer: do not pay for a 9% error, because copying last year's month already does better, and any tool the vendor proposes must beat that rule on a test year it has never seen. The rule leaves a visible gap, though: it cannot grow with the business. In Class 4 we build models that carry trend and seasonality together, and in Class 5 we make them automatic.
