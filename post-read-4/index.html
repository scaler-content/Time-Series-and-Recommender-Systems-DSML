Smooth Operators - Exponential Smoothing from Flat Lines to Holt-Winters

Build exponential smoothing models that carry a trend and a yearly pattern, choose between additive and multiplicative versions, reach a stated accuracy target, and deliver a forecast as a range and not as a single number.

Case: Lanternly144 months · Jan 2012 – Dec 2023Python · statsmodels ETS
About the dataLanternly is an online store selling festive lanterns and decorations. The series Units (lanterns and decorations sold per month) is clean: no gaps and no anomalies, using the steps from Class 1. The data is simulated for teaching; the numbers are not real figures of any company. Train = 2012–2022 (132 months), test = 2023. Smoothing recursions on this page run live in your browser; parameters that need an optimiser (the "estimated" values) and the ETS results come from the class's own statsmodels code.
1 · Cold open

The manager's three questions

Orders for stock go to the factory months ahead, and the warehouse hires temporary staff for the festive season. The operations manager has three questions:

1 · Patterns"Does your forecast understand how our business moves, the growth and the festive peak?"
2 · Accuracy"Will it be within 5% error on the last year of data?"
3 · Range"Can you also give me a lowest and a highest number, so purchasing knows how much to order and how much to keep as a buffer?"

These three questions are the whole story of this class and the next one. Look at the data first.

The series climbs from about 650 units a month in 2012 to more than 4,000 in the peak months of 2023, and every year has the same shape: low in the first half, a peak in October and November. Growth is usually 10% to 14%, but 2022 grew only 4.8%. Keep that year in mind, it comes back later.

The tools from Class 3 give the starting scores: split by time (train 2012–2022, test the last 12 months, 2023) and compare against baselines.

Visualizer · Class 3 tools on Lanternly

Seasonal naive has the right shape but is one year behind a business that grows 10% to 14% a year. SES has no shape at all: its α is 1.00, so it simply repeats December. Neither is near the 5% asked for.

What would a model need to know to fix both problems?
The growth (a trend) and the yearly shape (seasonality), and it should still learn them the way SES does: by giving more weight to recent data. That is Holt's method, and then Holt-Winters.

The plan

1 Add a trend to SES: Holt's method.
2 Add seasonality: Holt-Winters, with its three smoothing parameters.
3 Choose additive or multiplicative, and see what a wrong pairing costs.
4 Check the 5% target honestly.
5 Turn the point forecast into a range, and let the computer choose the model with AIC.
2 · Level and trend

Holt's method

SES keeps one number, the level ℓt: where the series is "now". Holt's method keeps a second number, the trend 𝑏t: how much the level moves per month. The forecast is the level plus ℎ steps of trend.

ŷt+h|t = ℓt + h·bt
ℓt = α·yt + (1−α)(ℓt−1 + bt−1)
bt = β*(ℓt − ℓt−1) + (1−β*)·bt−1

The term ℓt−1 + bt−1 is what the model expected the new level to be. The trend is a smoothed average of the recent changes in level, so it uses exponentially decaying weights as well. The level depends on the trend and the trend on the level: a recursion, which is why the model is also called double exponential smoothing (two smoothed quantities, each with its own decaying weights).

Think of a car on a highway. The level is the position, the trend is the speed. Every minute you get a new GPS reading. You do not jump to the GPS position (α=1) and you do not ignore it (α=0): you blend it with where you expected the car to be, and update your speed estimate the same way. The forecast is "position plus speed times the minutes ahead".

Visualizer · Holt lab
The recursion runs over the 2012–2022 training data in your browser, starting from the statsmodels initial level (≈ 630) and trend (≈ 20.5 units per month).

The model estimates its own parameters from the training data by minimising the squared one-step errors. It chooses α≈1 and β*=0, both on the edge of the allowed range. With α=1 the level jumps to every new observation; with β*=0 the trend never changes after its starting value. A value of exactly 0 or 1 is not a bug: the optimiser stopped at the boundary because the yearly swing is much larger than the trend, so the level has to chase the data. That is a clue that something is still missing, and Holt-Winters fixes it.

The equations are exactly the model: a short loop with the two update rules reproduces statsmodels' fitted values (np.allclose is True), including the final level of 3,337 and trend of 20.5.

for obs in train.values:
    one_step.append(level + slope)                     # forecast made before seeing obs
    new_level = a * obs + (1 - a) * (level + slope)    # level update
    slope = b * (new_level - level) + (1 - b) * slope  # trend update
    level = new_level

The forecast is a straight line rising by exactly 20.5 units every month, as ℓT + h·bT says. But its MAPE is 30.8%, slightly worse than SES (29.4%): the line starts from the December level, a month already high, and cannot rise and fall with the year. Adding a trend alone did not help because the festive swing is far bigger than the growth.

Is the trend useless then?
No. It helps when the seasonality is not in the way. On yearly totals, each year is one number and the seasonality has been added up and removed:

SES is off by 11.0% because it predicts "the same as last year"; Holt is off by only 5.0% because it carries the growth. Holt's method does its job: it captures the trend. What it cannot do is the monthly shape.

3 · Level, trend and seasonality

Holt-Winters

Now add a third number, the seasonal index 𝑠t, one for each position in the year. With 𝑚 = the number of seasons in a cycle (12 for monthly data with a yearly pattern), the additive Holt-Winters model is:

ŷt+h|t = ℓt + h·bt + st+h−m
ℓt = α(yt − st−m) + (1−α)(ℓt−1 + bt−1)
bt = β*(ℓt − ℓt−1) + (1−β*)·bt−1
st = γ(yt − ℓt−1 − bt−1) + (1−γ)·st−m

Read the level equation: before the level is updated, the seasonal effect is removed from the new observation. Read the seasonal equation: what is left of the observation after removing level and trend is new evidence about this month, blended with last year's index using the weight γ.

The shop keeps three notebooks. The first says where sales are now, the second how fast they are growing, and the third has twelve lines, one per month, saying how much above or below normal that month usually is. Every month the manager reads the new number and edits one line in each of the first two notebooks and the one line of the third for that month. A forecast is: where we are, plus the growth over the months ahead, plus the notebook line for the month being forecast.

With the additive estimate, α≈0.12, β*=0 and γ≈0.80. The level now reacts slowly, because the seasonal index takes care of the swings. The seasonal weight γ is large: the model keeps revising the monthly shape quickly. That number is a warning sign that returns in the next section. β* is exactly 0 again because the growth in units per month is stable over the 11 training years, so the optimiser sees no gain in revising it; the level equation carries the real changes.

The four equations are exactly the model: rebuilding the fitted values by hand matches statsmodels to about 10−12. The learned components agree with Class 2: the level climbs steadily, the trend is a flat line at about 14 units per month, and the seasonal component is a repeating wave. Read the indices as "how far this month sits from the trend line": November is +1,281 units above it and January −513 below it, but now they are updated month by month, with recent data counting most.

4 · The pairing

Additive vs multiplicative, and what goes wrong with a bad pairing

The trend and the seasonal term can each be additive or multiplicative. For a multiplicative seasonal term:

ŷt+h|t = (ℓt + h·bt) · st+h−m
ℓt = α · yt / st−m + (1−α)(ℓt−1 + bt−1),   st = γ · yt / (ℓt−1 + bt−1) + (1−γ)·st−m

The seasonal index is now a ratio around 1, and the trend equation stays the same. A multiplicative trend replaces ℓt + h·bt by ℓt·bth, where 𝑏t is a growth factor such as 1.01 for 1% growth per month.

An additive model says "every November sells 1,281 units more than normal". A multiplicative model says "every November sells 35% more than normal". If the shop is three times bigger than ten years ago, which sentence is more believable? The answer is in the data: does the size of the seasonal swing, in units, stay the same or grow with the level?

Visualizer · how the swing behaves

From 2012 to 2023 the swing between the best and worst month grows from 643 units to 2,007 units, about three times larger, while the ratio of peak to trough stays close to 2. That is the signature of multiplicative seasonality: the yearly shape is a percentage of the level, not a fixed number of units.

Fit all four pairings

Visualizer · four pairings, one training set
γ needed by each pairing

Look at γ and the training SSE before the test error. With additive seasonality the model needs γ ≈ 0.80 (or 0.78): it must rewrite the monthly shape almost every year to keep up with the growing swing, and its squared training error is about 1.32 million. With multiplicative seasonality γ falls to 0 — one stable shape of percentages is enough — and the training error is about 0.59 million, less than half. A bad pairing shows up in the parameters before it shows up in the forecast.

On the 2023 test year all four pairings are below 5%, so one year does not separate them well. Testing on three years, always training on the past only:

Visualizer · rolling test years

Averaged over the three years, additive/additive scores 6.21%, additive trend with multiplicative season 4.25% and multiplicative/multiplicative 4.16%: the multiplicative seasonal term wins in every year. 2022 is hard for every pairing (6.8% to 9.6%): growth fell to 4.8% that year, and no smoothing model could know in advance that growth would slow.

There is no universal rule for the pairing; the data decides, by looking at how the swing behaves. There is also a hard failure for the multiplicative terms: they need strictly positive data. Setting one training month to zero makes statsmodels raise ValueError: endog must be strictly positive when using multiplicative trend or seasonal components. A multiplicative seasonal term divides by the data, so it refuses to run when a month is zero; use the additive form, or fix the data.

5 · Accuracy

Reaching the MAPE ≤ 5% target

The second question is a number: a MAPE of at most 5% on the last year. The working model keeps it simple: additive trend with multiplicative season. Every model goes on one scoreboard with the same function and the same test year.

Visualizer · the scoreboard

Three models meet the target on 2023: HW with additive trend and multiplicative season (3.29%), the same with a multiplicative trend (2.69%) and the additive version (4.42%). Seasonal naive (10.9%), SES and Holt are far above 5%. The working model's MAE is 109 units, small against average monthly sales of about 2,900 in 2023.

A single MAPE hides the shape of the errors. Look month by month, and check the sign:

Visualizer · where the working model misses

Three months are above 5%: September (7.4%), October (10.5%) and December (6.5%), all in the run-up to and inside the festive season, while November is almost perfect (0.1%). The signed error is positive, so the model under-forecasts, by about 97 units a month, because 2023 grew faster (12.3%) than the slow 2022 that the model had just learned from.

The three-year test gives the honest answer: the target was met in 2 of the 3 years, and the year that missed (2022, 6.8%) is the year growth slowed from 13.9% to 4.8%. A forecaster should say: "Expect about 3% to 4% in a normal year, and expect a miss when the growth rate changes suddenly".

ImportantA target is a statement about the future, and one test year is one draw. Report the score on several test years, and report the months where the model is weakest, as the manager prepares for those months. This is why the third question, a range, matters: a point forecast cannot say how wrong it might be.
Could we tune α, β* and γ on the test year until the MAPE is below 3%?
No. That would be using the answer to choose the question, the same leak as in Class 3. The parameters are estimated on the training years only; the test year is touched only to score.

6 · Uncertainty

Confidence intervals: delivering a range forecast

A forecast of 4,177 units for November is a promise nobody can keep. A better statement is "between 3,851 and 4,499 units, with 95% confidence". The pair (L, U) is a prediction interval (often called a confidence interval in forecasting), and its job is to tell the user how uncertain the forecast is. For a model with normal errors:

ŷT+h ± z1−α/2 · σ̂h

σ̂h is the standard deviation of the forecast error ℎ steps ahead, and α is the significance level: the interval has confidence 1−α. For α = 0.05 it is a 95% interval and z = 1.96.

Is this α the same as the smoothing level α?
No, only the same letter. The smoothing level is a model parameter between 0 and 1 that controls memory. The significance level is a choice about how sure we want to be. In code: smoothing_level vs the alpha argument of the interval function.

Two facts: higher confidence means a wider interval (larger 𝑧), and the interval gets wider with the horizon (larger σ̂h), because errors accumulate. ExponentialSmoothing gives only point forecasts; ETSModel describes the same family of models but also the error, so the library can work out σ̂h. We start with ETS(A,A,M): additive error, additive trend, multiplicative season. It has a residual standard deviation of about 70 units and a 2023 MAPE of 3.32%, very close to the Holt-Winters model.

Visualizer · the hand formula
For January 2023: forecast 2,213.2, σ = 70.3 units.

At 95% the hand formula gives 2,075 to 2,351 and the library gives 2,073 to 2,351: a tiny gap, simulation noise plus the effect of the multiplicative season. A prediction interval is a forecast plus and minus a multiple of the typical error: 1.28 for 80%, 1.96 for 95%, 2.58 for 99%. For this model statsmodels finds the interval by simulation (5,000 futures, fixed seed).

Visualizer · range forecast for 2023

The band is clearly wider for the large festive months than for quiet months, and wider in month 12 than in month 1 (95%: 277 vs 575 units), because uncertainty grows with the horizon. As for coverage, the 80% interval holds 9 of 12 actuals (75%); the 95% and 99% hold 11. With only 12 test points the share inside cannot be expected to equal the nominal value exactly.

If we want a narrower band, what can we change?
Either accept a lower confidence, or build a better model. A smaller 𝑧 is free but gives up the promise: a narrower interval contains the actual value less often.
ImportantThe interval is not a worst case. A 95% interval is expected to miss in about 1 month out of 20. For stock decisions the manager chooses the confidence that matches the cost of a stock-out against the cost of extra stock.

7 · Automatic choice

Automatic model choice with ETSModel and AIC

So far the pairing was chosen by looking at the swing. With ETSModel the computer can compare all the options. Every exponential smoothing model is named by three letters: Error, Trend and Seasonal, each N (none), A (additive) or M (multiplicative). A trend can also be damped (Ad), which flattens the slope over time. ETS(A,N,N) is SES, ETS(A,A,N) is Holt, and ETS(A,A,M) is the model of the last section.

AIC = 2k − 2 ln L̂

L̂ is the maximised likelihood (how well the model explains the training data) and 𝑘 the number of estimated parameters, including the 12 starting seasonal values. The first term punishes complexity and the second rewards fit. Lower is better. For ETS(A,A,M): log-likelihood −748.76 and 𝑘 = 18, so 2·18 − 2·(−748.76) = 1,533.52, exactly the library's AIC. The 18 parameters are three smoothing values, the starting level and trend, twelve starting seasonal values, and the error variance.

Visualizer · AIC vs test MAPE
model with a seasonal part (20)no seasonal part (10)lowest AIC

The winner is ETS(M,A,M): multiplicative error, additive trend, multiplicative season. It agrees with the by-hand finding that the swing is proportional to the level, and goes a step further: the noise is proportional to the level too (a 5% miss in a big month is bigger in units than in a small month). All ten models without a seasonal part sit in positions 20 to 30. The lowest AIC does not have the lowest test MAPE.

Among the 20 models with a seasonal part, the rank correlation between AIC and test MAPE is 0.55: AIC points in the right general direction but does not predict the winner of one test year. That is expected: AIC judges fit on the training years with a complexity penalty, and a single test year of 12 points is noisy. The three worst models by AIC (ETS(A,A,N), ETS(A,Ad,N), ETS(A,Md,N)) all lack seasonality, with test MAPEs of 18% to 19%.

ImportantAIC is a tool for shortlisting, not an oracle. Use it to throw away models with no seasonal part and to prefer simple ones among models with similar scores, then confirm on several test years as in Section 4. AIC values can only be compared between models fitted to the same data.

The final range forecast

Visualizer · two ranges compared

ETS(M,A,M) gives a 2023 MAPE of 3.45%, a little higher than the 3.32% of ETS(A,A,M), and its 95% interval also holds 11 of 12 actuals. The three questions are now answered. Patterns: a model with level, trend and a multiplicative yearly shape. Accuracy: about 3% on 2023, and under 5% in two of three test years. Range: a monthly low and high that widen with the size of the month and the horizon.

Recap

Key takeaways

  • Holt's method adds a trend to SES: the forecast is ℓT + h·bT, a straight line. It fixes the growth but not the yearly shape, so on strongly seasonal data it can still score badly.
  • Holt-Winters has three smoothing parameters: α for the level, β* for the trend and γ for the seasonal indices. Each is updated by the same idea: a weighted mix of new evidence and old belief.
  • The pairing can be additive or multiplicative. If the size of the swing grows with the level, use multiplicative seasonality. A bad pairing shows up as a high γ and a large training error, and multiplicative terms fail on zeros.
  • A target such as MAPE ≤ 5% should be checked on several test years and by month, not on a single score, and never tuned on the test year.
  • A prediction interval is the forecast ± z·σ̂h. Higher confidence gives a wider band, and a longer horizon gives a wider band. The interval's α is not the smoothing α.
  • ETSModel names models by Error, Trend and Season, and AIC (2k − 2 ln L̂) ranks them on the training data. Use it to shortlist, then confirm on test years.

Common mistakes

Reading the best test MAPE of one year as proof that a model is the best.
Using a multiplicative seasonal term on data with zeros or negative values.
Confusing the significance level α of an interval with the smoothing level α.
Comparing AIC values of models fitted on different data.
Delivering only a point forecast to a manager who has to order stock.
Tuning smoothing parameters by looking at the test set.
Closing the first half of the Lanternly storyThe manager has what was asked: a model that follows growth and the festive peak, a score of about 3% on the last year, and a monthly range. One question remains, and the 2022 miss pointed at it: what is left in the data after the model has taken out level, trend and season? If what is left is pure noise, the model is complete; if it still has structure, we can improve it. To answer this we need to know when a series is predictable by itself.
